# About — long-form narrative

This is the personal-statement-grade version: the one that gets adapted for grad school admissions, fellowships, podcast intros, and the "tell me about yourself" question when the audience has 5+ minutes.

The voice is first-person. The arc is: **values → arc → vision**.

---

## The motto

*I engineer tenacity.*

When the Penn State College of Engineering filmed me for the University's *I Engineer* series, the producer asked for one word that characterized my progression. *Tenacity* — intense and persistent diligence — was what enabled a 4.0 in honors mechanical engineering, an undefeated D1 club boxing career, two 100+ student clubs run simultaneously in my senior year, and an honors thesis. It is now what carries my graduate research at UC Berkeley.

## How I see curiosity and creativity

My perspective on both is centered around **action**.

Curiosity, a strong desire to learn and explore, becomes valuable through exploration. Inactive curiosity doesn't create progress. For me, applied curiosity means putting myself into challenging and unfamiliar environments — a propellant lab as a freshman, a German engineering school for a summer, a boxing club where I'm publicly bad in front of fifty people.

Creativity is more than imagination producing original work. It is a skill cultivated by exposure and iteration. Applied creativity, for me, involves **carefully crafting ideas, boldly implementing them, and reimagining them without becoming fixated.**

I have done this in engineering leadership, community building, and combat sports.

## Where I come from

Raised in a Caribbean immigrant family by a single mother who worked as an engineer at NASA. I recognized early the value of a diverse, creative perspective in advancing public interest. My value system is inspired by my parents — both decorated engineering careers at NASA, both of whom worked for public interest, not profit. They turned down higher-paying jobs to have a more service-focused career, advancing the country's scientific and technological capabilities through research projects that were uncertain and nonlucrative. I deeply value that approach and seek to continue that legacy.

## What I've built (intellectual merit)

I sought multiple learning opportunities through classroom learning, hands-on jobs, and research internships. Each shaped my trajectory and research interests.

- In the classroom: graduate-level coursework on designing for advanced manufacturing, study abroad in Singapore (product development) and Germany (AI and manufacturing).
- On-campus jobs: three years as an Autodesk Fusion 360 design educator on campus, three years at Penn State's Learning Factory — a 100,000 ft² makerspace — first training students on 3D printers, mills, lathes, CNC machines and laser cutters; later building an asset-management system and creating a special-projects role from scratch.
- Research: Purdue's Zucrow Labs (DARPA propellant ML, freshman year), Penn State's Applied Research Lab (composite-steel bolted-joint dynamics, honors thesis), Rivian (single-piece die-cast door redesign supporting a patent application).

The Rivian summer was where my PhD vision crystallized. The door problem — replacing a stamped sheet-metal assembly with a single cast inner panel — was rewarding precisely because the design-for-manufacturing problem-solving process was so painfully manual. Around the same time I discovered Ryan McClelland's evolved-structures work at NASA Goddard: a manufacturable generative-design part made in one hour that outperformed the work of two engineers over three days. I had the epiphany that frames my dissertation: **by the time I have my PhD, DFM projects like the Rivian door won't take months — they'll take days.**

## What I've built (broader impacts)

In preparing for graduate school, I left a legacy at Penn State by threading my experiences and skills together over a multi-year effort.

- **First year:** Led the creation of an introductory manufacturing project that is now an official module taken by 200+ new students each year.
- **Sophomore year on:** Founded the first multifaceted engineering design team at Penn State within ASME — focused on welcoming students whose ideas didn't fit elsewhere, including students from underrepresented backgrounds. The first attempt failed. I started from scratch the next semester, focused on value to members rather than organization growth, and grew it across two years to 80 members and four projects.
- **Junior year:** Built a pipeline with NSBE for inexperienced students from non-technical clubs to learn basic CAD and add to their portfolios.
- **Senior year:** As ASME president, ran the 18-person executive board, oversaw the now 10-person design team board, launched a corporate sponsorship program that raised thousands of dollars, organized 60+ events in two semesters, and rebuilt the 28-person leadership structure. ASME membership grew from 40 to 120 people during my tenure.
- **Senior year, in parallel:** As DiscipleMakers president, led 100+ members across 10 small groups, rebuilt the leadership training program, gave two keynote speeches, and grew my own discussion group from a typical 6 members to 20+. Received the *Bold Leader* Award when I graduated.

The thread: I make impact that outlives my presence. I trained the next leaders. ASME and DM both kept growing after I left.

## The boxing arc

I dropped out of high school cross country in my senior year because it was too hard. Combat sports were the thing I was most afraid of. That became the reason to do them.

- 10 months of training six days a week at a club. Still bad.
- Tried out for the Penn State team. Lost composure in the ring. Got cut.
- Walk-ons could attend the conditioning test even after being cut. No one had done it before — there was no reason to. Unless you were trying to make a point. I went. Outperformed several people who *had* made the team, won one of the events.
- Year two: trained with such focus the question shifted from *if* to *when*. Made the team. Retired **3–0**. **Coaches' Choice 2025** award. 3rd-fastest runner on the team. Unofficial team tutor. Helped two national runners-up prepare for their championship fights.
- Normally engineers pull back from sports as classes get harder. I didn't.

## What I'm doing at Berkeley

PhD in the **Co-Design Lab** with **Prof. Kosa Goucher-Lambert**, in collaboration with **Autodesk Research** and the **Berkeley Institute of Design**. The dissertation: a co-creation framework for human–AI–machinist collaboration in early-stage CAD/CAM, tested empirically and instantiated as agentic models trained on collected data. Three aims:

1. Identify the core problems behind the slow design-manufacturing handoff and create a co-creation framework (field study + collaboration study).
2. Develop agentic co-creation methodologies and use them to collect training data through digital design aids — machinability rubrics, qualitative-feedback tools, geometry-assessment tools.
3. Train agentic co-creation models on that data and test implementation strategies — graph neural networks, agentic LLMs, 2D/3D CNNs, vision–language models.

I want this work to be open-source so that government organizations and national labs across the country can implement and improve it. The DoD critical-technology areas of *Trusted AI* and *Human-Machine Interfaces* are directly affected.

## Long-term vision

I aim to be a multidisciplinary leader who trailblazes innovation in the design and manufacturing of complex parts and systems. Whether the future role is professor, founder, or R&D director is still unknown.

What is known: I want to **create technology that lasts longer than my name and my generation** — impacting society for decades by equipping future engineers to do better work than I did. The shape of that is bringing experts in cognitive science, AI, user-centered design, manufacturing, and business together to develop powerful, widely accessible open-source design methods, algorithms, and systems. Not just for tomorrow's experts — also for those with fewer resources and advantages, so engineering capability becomes more accessible.

## How I work

- **Servant leadership.** Not being in charge — benefitting those in my charge. Train people in the skills I lack instead of cloning myself. Step back when the team is ready. Both ASME and DM kept growing after I handed off.
- **Tough feedback, generous encouragement.** That's the culture I built into the ASME design team and what I bring into the lab.
- **Iteration over perfection.** After every full-team event we discussed how it went, made changes, applied them the next week. Same approach to research.
- **Failure as data.** First semester of ASME failed. First boxing tryout failed. Both became the foundation for what worked next.
- **Patience over multi-year arcs.** Most of what I'm proud of took 2–4 years to mature. The PhD plan does too.
