# Signature stories

The set of anecdotes that get used in interviews, podcasts, application essays, and "tell me about a time when…" questions. Each one is rehearsed, has a tight version (30–60s spoken) and a long version, and a clear *what it shows about you* tagline.

---

## 1. The first ASME launch failed — and that was the foundation

**Tagline:** Failure as data. Iterate the strategy, not the goal.

**30-second version:**
> I founded an engineering design team at Penn State as a sophomore. The first semester it failed completely — my founding partners ghosted me, the project made no progress. The next semester I started over: new team, new branding, but the real change was strategy. I'd been leading with the org's growth; I switched to leading with value to members. Two years later we had 80 members across four projects.

**Long version:**
*Use the full Hertz / GRFP narrative — see [about.md](about.md) "What I've built (broader impacts)" section. The arc is: vision → undisciplined first attempt → public failure → restart from scratch → strategy change (members > org) → traction (5 officers, 20 members) → stability (40 members, 2 projects) → expansion (Diversity Roundtable, NSBE pipeline) → handoff to ASME-wide presidency → 80 members peak.*

**What it shows:** Resilience, strategic adaptation, capacity for multi-year arcs, willingness to learn from failure publicly.

**Where to use it:**
- "Tell me about a time you failed."
- "Tell me about a time you led a team."
- "What do you do when a plan isn't working?"
- Fellowship broader-impacts narrative.

## 2. Walked on to the conditioning test after being cut

**Tagline:** Show up to the test you've already failed.

**30-second version:**
> I tried out for the Penn State boxing team and got cut at the first round. The rules said cut walk-ons could still attend the conditioning test — a four-hour grinder no one had bothered to do after being cut, because there was no point. I went anyway. I outperformed several people who *had* made the team and won one of the events. The next year I made the team and retired 3-0 with the Coaches' Choice award.

**Long version:**
*Use the Hertz "Sweet Science" essay structure — see [about.md](about.md). Cross-country dropout in high school → 10 months at a boxing club six days a week → first tryout, lost composure, cut → walk-on to the conditioning test "to make a point" → outperformed team members → year 2: trained until "if" became "when" → second tryout, made the team → 3-0 → Coaches' Choice 2025 → coach admitted he wished he'd let me on a year earlier.*

**What it shows:** Tenacity (the headline trait), willingness to perform after failure, intrinsic standards independent of the result.

**Where to use it:**
- Character-revealing moments.
- "When was a time you weren't sure you could do something but did it anyway?"
- The "tenacity" answer — only when the audience values character over technical chops.

## 3. The Rivian door — when the dissertation idea crystallized

**Tagline:** The right problem reveals itself when you're inside the wrong process.

**30-second version:**
> At Rivian I was a side-closures intern. The team wanted to replace a welded sheet-metal door inner with a single die-cast part. I designed the inner panel and impact beam in CATIA, simulated door sag in Simsolid, did the FMEA, built the business case. The solution supported a patent application and saved millions in tooling. But the deeper thing was the *process* — months of expensive iteration, with manufacturability bolted on at the end. Around the same time I read Ryan McClelland's evolved-structures work at NASA Goddard — a generative-design part made in one hour that beat two engineers over three days. That's when I knew: by the time I have my PhD, projects like the Rivian door won't take months. They'll take days. That's what I'm building.

**What it shows:** Technical depth, ability to convert real industry pain into a research thesis, narrative arc from practitioner to researcher.

**Where to use it:**
- Research-statement opening.
- "Why did you go to grad school?"
- "How did you choose your dissertation topic?"
- Industry-recruiter conversation about why you're doing the PhD instead of staying in industry.

## 4. The DM small-group experiment — leading by demonstrating

**Tagline:** Lead by being the proof of concept.

**30-second version:**
> When I became president of DiscipleMakers as a senior, I wanted to change the small-group model — make groups more welcoming and discussion-driven. I couldn't just mandate it. So I ran my own group on the new model. Average DM group size was about 6. Mine grew to 20+. The members started independently modeling the welcoming culture for the rest of the org. By the time I graduated, the new model had been adopted across DM, and I got the Bold Leader Award.

**What it shows:** Leadership as demonstration, not directive. Strategic patience. Operational humility — "remain a student of those you want to teach."

**Where to use it:**
- "Tell me about a time you changed an organization's culture."
- "How do you lead without authority?"
- Servant-leadership philosophy questions.

## 5. "I am still a mechanical engineer, not a programmer."

**Tagline:** Honest framing of where the work sits.

**30-second version:**
> The reality of my research is that I'm a mechanical engineer who uses computational tools — not a programmer who studies machines. The dissertation isn't about inventing new neural-network architectures from scratch. It's about *applying* existing computational systems to physical tasks that remain underutilized. There's an abundance of intelligent systems with incredible potential to make physical things, and that's what I intend to realize.

**What it shows:** Self-aware positioning, intellectual honesty, an understanding of where the genuine contribution sits.

**Where to use it:**
- Hertz-style philosophical questions about field choice.
- When an ML researcher pushes on whether you're "an AI person."
- Pre-empting the "is this CS or ME" question.

## 6. The single mom NASA engineer

**Tagline:** Public-interest research is a value system, not a job description.

**30-second version:**
> My value system comes from my parents, both of whom had decorated NASA engineering careers. They turned down higher-paying jobs to do public-interest work — research projects that were uncertain and nonlucrative. My mom raised me as a single parent while doing that. I want to continue that legacy, which is why my PhD output is going to be open source, and why I'm focused on tools that don't just help tomorrow's experts but also people with fewer resources.

**What it shows:** Origin of values, motivation behind open-source orientation, lineage in mission-driven engineering.

**Where to use it:**
- Personal statements (sparingly — used in GRFP).
- Mission-aligned org conversations (Hertz, Ford, GEM, NASA).
- "Why this field?" follow-ups.

## 7. The 700-student Fusion adoption

**Tagline:** Long-quiet effort that compounds.

**30-second version:**
> I worked as Penn State's Autodesk Fusion 360 Champion for three years. I just kept running workshops, helped students, embedded into the design team. By my senior year I'd grown student Fusion usage by 500% — out-performing all previous Penn State ambassadors. Then the School of Engineering Design adopted Fusion as the official software for the introductory design course — 700 students per year. The design team's CAD nights are now part of the course's official syllabus. None of that was a single big initiative. It was three years of patient, weekly compounding.

**What it shows:** Comfort with multi-year compounding effort. The kind of work that scales after you leave.

**Where to use it:**
- "Tell me about an underrated impact you've had."
- "What's a project you're proud of that wasn't flashy?"

## 8. The 0.003" tolerance machined steering parts

**Tagline:** I can do the grunt work.

**30-second version:**
> When I led the Formula SAE steering subsystem, I didn't outsource the manufacturing. I machined the circular components within 0.003-inch tolerance myself, on a manual lathe. I 3D-printed mockups for proof-of-concept and set up the welding fixture for another teammate. While I was doing all this, I cut the system's bill of materials in half with the same functionality.

**What it shows:** Technical credibility — not just a project manager. Someone who can do the work.

**Where to use it:**
- Industry-job interviews where hands-on credibility matters.
- "What's the most technical thing you've personally built?"
- Defense against being seen as only a leader, not an engineer.

## 9. Three jobs simultaneously

**Tagline:** Capacity is real and provable.

**30-second version (from the SpaceX cover letter):**
> While writing my honors thesis I was holding three jobs at once — Penn State's Applied Research Lab on bolted-joint dynamics, the Learning Factory teaching 3D printing and machining, and Autodesk teaching Fusion 360. I built trust with each manager to the point of working full part-time shifts without checking in. I can create results with little outside motivation or structure.

**What it shows:** Self-direction, time discipline, multi-thread capacity.

**Where to use it:**
- Industry interviews — particularly ones that test capacity for ambiguous, demanding work.
- The deliberately unconventional cover letter (see SpaceX template).
- "Tell me about a time you managed competing priorities."

## 10. Started boxing because the only barrier was difficulty

**Tagline:** The thing you're avoiding because it's hard becomes the proof you can do hard things.

**30-second version:**
> The reason I started boxing was that I realized the *only* thing preventing me from combat training was its difficulty. So I had to do it. I was awful in front of fifty people in the boxing club. The first ten months I was still pretty bad. But my ability to handle stress changed. The harder boxing got, the more peaceful college got — the pressure of getting punched made academic stress feel proportional.

**What it shows:** Self-awareness, the discipline of choosing difficulty, transferability of stress regulation to research.

**Where to use it:**
- Personal-statement bridges between unrelated arcs.
- "Tell me something not on your CV."
- When character matters more than credentials.

## Anti-stories (avoid these)

- **The full grad-school admissions arc.** Too many people tell that story; you got in, that's enough.
- **High school anything** — except as a one-line setup ("I dropped out of cross country my senior year").
- **Other people's failures** — never punching down on past collaborators or coaches who cut you.
- **Religion as a wedge.** DiscipleMakers is referenced for the leadership and apprenticeship framing; the theological content stays out of professional contexts unless explicitly invited.
